About

A scholar-practitioner's route into this question

I came to this research the long way — through the classroom, not the seminar room. Before I studied how special education systems work, I ran one: as a resource teacher in Cameroon writing IEPs by hand for pupils with dyslexia and ADHD, and later as an instructor at a Massachusetts autism school applying ABA principles one student at a time. That decade of direct practice is the lens I bring to my doctoral work at Boston University's Wheelock College — a persistent, practical question about whether the current wave of AI-powered personalized learning tools genuinely helps teachers reach the students who are hardest to reach, or simply promises to. I hold that question with both technical rigor and real skepticism, which I think is exactly what it deserves.

Research

A three-paper dissertation, from evidence to implementation

My dissertation, Evidence, Perceptions, and Experiences in the Implementation of Artificial Intelligence-Assisted Personalized Learning for Students with Learning Disabilities, is built as a deliberate progression rather than three disconnected studies: first establishing what the existing evidence actually shows, then measuring whether U.S. K-12 teachers are ready and willing to adopt these tools, and finally listening closely to the special education teachers already doing it. Alongside this, I contribute to two federally funded IES research grants under Dr. Nancy J. Nelson, working directly with early-grade teachers implementing intensive reading interventions for students at risk of dyslexia.

Paper I
Systematic Review & Meta-Analysis
Standardized effect sizes and moderator analysis of AI-assisted personalized learning interventions on reading and math outcomes for K-12 students with specific learning disabilities.
Paper II
Teacher Adoption Survey (TAM)
Cross-sectional survey applying the Technology Acceptance Model to U.S. K-12 teachers' usage patterns, perceived usefulness, and ethical concerns around generative AI for personalized instruction.
Paper III
Qualitative Case Study (Intelligent-TPACK)
Case study of special education teachers already adopting or planning to adopt generative AI tools to support students with learning disabilities.
2023 – Present
Graduate Research Assistant, Wheelock College, Boston University
Two funded IES grants under Dr. Nancy J. Nelson: a pilot intensive reading intervention within MTSS-R for students at risk of dyslexia, and a randomized controlled trial of the NumberShire Level K math intervention. Mixed-methods analysis of survey and interview data from 18 PreK–2 teacher interventionists across five school districts.
Teaching

From supporting the classroom to leading it

My teaching has moved in a clear arc — from supporting instruction to co-designing and delivering it — and each step has stayed close to the same core conviction: methods coursework only matters when it's tied tightly to the decisions teachers actually face. I've mentored master's students through the full arc of research methodology, and I now co-teach practicing K-12 teachers in applying Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports as both a conceptual and organizational framework, not just a compliance checklist.

Practice

A decade in the classroom, on two continents

Before Boston University, I spent five years as a special education resource teacher at St. Rita Inclusive Primary School in Buea, Cameroon, writing and adapting IEPs for elementary pupils with learning disabilities and ADHD, and building the kind of home–school partnerships that keep at-risk learners from falling through the cracks. More recently, at the May Center School for Autism and Developmental Disabilities in Massachusetts, I applied Applied Behavior Analysis directly — reinforcement, task analysis, data-driven decision-making — with children and adolescents with moderate and severe autism. This is the ground truth my research keeps returning to.
